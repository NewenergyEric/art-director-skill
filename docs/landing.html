A NOTE FROM ERIC

A story about an
art-director friend.

Of everyone I know, he is one of the people I would most readily call an artist.

In games, a lot of conversations are about pipelines, tools and efficiency. He talks about how a group of people can speak to one another through something they make. He cares about that elusive thing: how it feels.

For a while, we met often for meals or coffee. Whenever work came up, he had the same complaint: he had spent another whole day explaining “what we’re trying to make” to a new teammate, an outsourcing partner, or another AI that was becoming better at following instructions and worse at making its own judgments.

Read the rest of the story

I asked him, “Which part actually frustrates you?”

He thought for a moment. “It’s not that their work is bad. It’s that I have to explain everything from the beginning, every time. I finish explaining one version, and then I have to start again with the next. And once making a game becomes pure engineering—processes, parameters, checklists—the artistry, the playfulness, the fun starts to disappear.”

That stayed with me.

I’m a game lover and an FDE—a forward-deployed engineer. My work is about helping people connect the pieces. I realized that his problem had never been his taste. His taste was good. The problem was that he couldn’t hand it over or reuse it. It lived in his head, and in those few minutes when he explained it all again.

So we talked some more and did a few interviews. We gradually drew out the judgments behind his work: the references, the samples, what could change and what had to stay. We shaped them into something another person, or even an AI, could work from.

It doesn’t design for him or replace his taste. It gives that “feeling” a form he can pass on.

Later, he actually started using it.

That’s why I made a “visual memory”: to keep an artist’s sensibility from being swallowed by engineering.

Start in your agent.

Use an Agent Skills-compatible tool such as Codex, Claude Code or Cursor. A solo creator can take both roles below.

npx skills add https://github.com/NewenergyEric/art-director-skill --skill art-director

Core helpers need Python 3.10+. You can also download the Skill ZIP and ask your agent to install it.

01

I’m setting
the art direction.

Put existing work, references and review notes in a folder your agent can read. Start with what you have.

Use $art-director. Here are our designs, references and notes. Draft our art direction, then help me define the character, environment and UI requirements. Prepare samples for me to review and package the result for the team.

You get A substantive draft, decisions needing your input, a sample plan and a shareable project pack.

02

I’m creating
within the rules.

Give your agent the director’s complete project pack. It contains the project’s rules and materials.

Read this project pack. Extend the lamp family by two levels. Keep its camera, light and materials; make each upgrade distinct in silhouette. Show me a first sample, the rules you used and the checks still open.

You get A versioned task, relevant references, a first sample and a check record. Creation uses the tools available in your agent.

Keep the conversation going: Check these assets against the rules. Show me what needs changing and why.

Which of the two Skills should I use?

art-director establishes direction. The optional game-art-production organizes generation, assembly and layered delivery from the same pack. Compositor is an optional layer editor, not a generator or animation runtime. Production Skill ZIP

Where are the earlier studies and layered files?

The frozen Tidekeeper 0.3.0 pack preserves the earlier scene recipes and Compositor export. It predates the eight-frame repair action in 0.4.0. Format and relocation checks passed; native Mac opening remains untested. 0.3.0 candidate ZIP · 0.3.0 evidence · Earlier visual studies

Can I build on an existing design system?

Yes. The agent starts with your current rules and samples, identifies gaps and helps you fill them. Discuss changes naturally; the agent keeps the guides and project pack in sync.

Who decides whether the design is good?

The director owns direction and release decisions. The Skill organizes the evidence, checks against the rules and explains revisions. Passing a technical check is separate from visual approval.

What happens when the project changes?

The director publishes a new version. New tasks use it; existing tasks keep their original versions so their rules do not shift midway.
